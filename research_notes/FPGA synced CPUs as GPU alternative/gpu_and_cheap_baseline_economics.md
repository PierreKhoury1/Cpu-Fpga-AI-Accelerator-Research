# GPU and cheap-AI-inference baseline economics (market state as of 27 October 2026)

Scope: the quantitative baseline that a proposed "cheaper than GPU" CPU+FPGA inference design must beat. All prices USD. "List" = vendor-published; "street" = retailer/marketplace asking price; "used" = secondary-market asking or tracker price (completed-sale data is scarce, see Gaps). Peak TOPS/TFLOPS are labelled dense or sparse wherever the source labels them; NVIDIA marketing pages quote sparse numbers (2x dense). Every number is dated to its source. Derived ratios (GB/s per dollar, $/M tokens) are in the Inferences subsections and show their arithmetic.

## Key Question 1: Datacenter accelerators — specs, purchase price, cloud rental (H100/H200/B200/B300, RTX PRO 6000, L40S, L4, A100, MI300X/MI325X/MI355X, Gaudi 3)

### Takeaway
Datacenter inference in late 2026 is dominated by Hopper (H100/H200, 3.35–4.8 TB/s) renting at a ~$2/GPU-hr neocloud floor (~$3.3–3.7 median, $8–13 at hyperscalers) and Blackwell (B200/B300, 8 TB/s HBM3e) renting from ~$3.75 (spot/in-stock floor) to ~$8 (median) per GPU-hour; purchase prices for new H100s remain $25k–$40k and used H100s span a very wide $6k–$28k depending on source and form factor. Rental prices rose during 2026 (+24% y/y for H100 medians), so the cloud $/hr baseline is firmer, not softer, than a year ago.

### Inferences
- Dense peak numbers to use for the baseline (converted from NVIDIA's sparse marketing figures): H100 SXM ≈ 1,979 TFLOPS dense FP8 / 989 dense BF16; B200 ≈ 4,500 dense FP8 / 9,000 dense FP4; B300 ≈ 4,500 dense FP8 (SemiAnalysis) / 13,500 dense FP4. AMD publishes dense numbers directly (MI300X/MI325X 2,615; MI355X 5,033 dense FP8).
- The "cheap datacenter" baseline for a CPU+FPGA design is not list price but the neocloud floor: ~$2/hr for 3.35 TB/s (H100) and ~$3.75–6/hr for 8 TB/s (B200). A used H100 at ~$15k–$22k with a 4-year life at 70% utilization amortizes to roughly $0.6–0.9/hr before power and hosting, which is why neoclouds can offer $2/hr.
- Hyperscaler rates (2–3x neocloud) are irrelevant as a competitive bar; anyone cost-sensitive already rents from neoclouds or buys used.

## Key Question 2: Consumer/prosumer GPUs — specs, 2026 street/used prices, rental, measured llama.cpp throughput

### Takeaway
Consumer GPUs are the real bandwidth-per-dollar leaders but 2026's DRAM/GDDR shortage has pushed street prices to 2–4x MSRP for the RTX 5090 (~$4,200 low to ~$7,400–8,500 typical in Oct 2026 versus $1,999 MSRP) and roughly doubled used RTX 4090/3090 prices; the used RTX 3090 (24 GB, 936 GB/s, ~$700–1,200) and the rental RTX 4090 at ~$0.34/hr remain the cheapest widely-supported CUDA options. On an identical llama.cpp Llama-2-7B Q4_0 test the RTX 5090 generates ~300 tok/s, RTX 4090 ~189, RTX 3090 ~162, RTX 3060 12 GB ~77, and an H100 ~281 — i.e., single-stream decode tracks memory bandwidth, not datacenter compute.

## Key Question 4: Measured LLM throughput, cost per million tokens, and API price ceilings

## Key Question 5: Performance-per-dollar and bandwidth-per-dollar by device class (derived)

### Takeaway
Measured on purchase price, used consumer GPUs and dead-ended used datacenter cards deliver the most memory bandwidth per dollar (used RTX 3090 ≈0.8–1.3 GB/s per $, MI50 ≈2–5 GB/s per $), new consumer flagships at 2026 street prices have fallen to ≈0.25–0.45 GB/s per $, Apple M5 Ultra and Blackwell datacenter parts sit at ≈0.16–0.27 GB/s per $, and the 128 GB LPDDR5X mini systems are the worst at ≈0.05–0.13 GB/s per $. Any CPU+FPGA design priced on commodity DDR5 must clear roughly 1 GB/s per dollar *of effective decode bandwidth* to beat a used 3090 and ~0.3 GB/s per $ to beat a street-price 5090 or an M5 Ultra.

## Key Question 6: GPU supply and price trend in 2026 — is performance getting cheaper?

### Takeaway
In 2026 GPUs did not get cheaper per unit of performance at the point of purchase: a DRAM/GDDR shortage (consumer GDDR6 spot ~$2.5 → ~$7.5/GB) pushed NVIDIA to three GeForce price increases (+10–15% in January, a 5090-only hike in May, a rumored 20–30% in August), AMD to +10% kit prices in July, NVIDIA to up-to-101% Jetson increases, Apple and NVIDIA to +$700–1,500 on unified-memory boxes, and the RTX PRO 6000 to nearly double ($8,565 → $16,000); datacenter rental prices also firmed (+24% y/y H100 median) rather than continuing the 2024–25 slide, with only used H100/A100 purchase prices clearly lower than their peaks. Cost per *token* nonetheless keeps falling because Blackwell's per-GPU throughput gains (10–65x on MoE at scale per SemiAnalysis) and software outrun hardware price inflation.

### Inferences
- For a hardware proposal the relevant trend is split: *hardware* $/GB/s rose in 2026 (consumer and unified-memory classes +15–100%, datacenter rental +24%), while *service* $/token kept falling through Blackwell throughput and software. A CPU+FPGA design built on commodity DDR5 is exposed to the same DRAM inflation (DDR5 contract prices rose with the shortage) but not to GDDR7/HBM/CoWoS scarcity, which is the supply-chain argument Yuan & Long make.
- The best time-stable comparison anchors are: used RTX 3090 ($700–1,200, flat-to-up in 2026), H100 neocloud floor (~$2/hr, flat since mid-2026), B200 in-stock floor (~$3.75/hr), and open-model API prices (8B ≈ $0.04/M out, 70B ≈ $0.32/M out). The design should be scored against these four, dated October 2026.
- Because 2026 price increases were memory-driven, a design whose bill of materials is dominated by DRAM gets no relief from the shortage; its advantage must come from using cheaper DRAM *types* (DDR5 vs GDDR7/HBM) and from not paying for stranded compute.
