# FPGA-synced Xeons cannot undercut GPUs on inference

*Research report, 27 October 2026. Prices are USD, October 2026 unless stated. Every figure is labelled measured, peak, vendor claim or estimate, as in the research notes under `research_notes/FPGA synced CPUs as GPU alternative/`. The prototype design and simulation it summarises live in [docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md](../docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md) and [docs/sim_results.md](../docs/sim_results.md), generated by [tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py](../tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py) and [tools/inference_cost_model.py](../tools/inference_cost_model.py).*

No: an FPGA that synchronises several CPUs cannot make them behave like a GPU for AI inference at lower cost, because the fabric changes neither of the two quantities that decide the outcome. Decoding a large language model at batch 1 is a streaming problem, roughly **40 GB of weights read from memory for every token of a 70B 4-bit model**, so tokens per second is memory bandwidth divided by bytes, and the cost of that bandwidth is what the buyer pays for. The simulated four-node Intel Xeon 6980P cluster sketched in this project reaches **21–41 tok/s on Llama-70B 4-bit at $136,000 of hardware** with an FPGA all-reduce fabric, only **3 % faster** than the same hosts on off-the-shelf 400G RDMA NICs, against **29–36 tok/s from two used RTX 4090s at $7,000**, 55–68 tok/s from one H100 at $30,000 and 130–160 tok/s from one B200 at $46,000 (all simulator P10–P90 bands, calibrated against seven measured points). Per million generated tokens at batch 16 the Xeon cluster costs **$6–12** versus $0.8–1.0 on an H100, $0.5–0.6 on a B200 and $0.32 at a public API. The fabric is not the problem: the 160 all-reduces a 70B token needs take about 2 ms over RDMA and 1 ms through an FPGA tree, while streaming the weights from four sockets of DDR5 at the measured 32–54 % utilisation takes about 32 ms, and a 12-channel DDR5 socket now costs **$40–60 per GB/s of bandwidth** against about $1–2 for a used RTX 3090 and $4.5 for a Mac Studio M5 Ultra. What does lower the cost of inference, and is buildable today, is every design that puts the weights behind cheap high-bandwidth memory and uses the CPU for capacity: a Xeon host plus one GPU for mixture-of-experts models (ktransformers), used consumer GPUs, Apple's M5 Ultra, Intel's own Gaudi 3, batched serving on rented GPUs, and, if an FPGA must be in the design, an HBM FPGA that does the arithmetic itself, which is what Positron ships and what Positron is replacing with an ASIC because the FPGA is the expensive part.

## In October 2026 a used RTX 3090 buys 30–60x more bandwidth per dollar than a Xeon socket

The landscape question has a simple answer once every part is scored on the two numbers decode cares about, bytes per second of memory bandwidth and dollars per unit of it, with peak arithmetic as a secondary figure that matters for prefill and batching. Intel's Xeon 6980P is the best CPU candidate on paper: 128 cores with AMX, 12 DDR5 channels, **614 GB/s theoretical at DDR5-6400 or 845 GB/s with MRDIMM-8800**, a list price of $17,800 and 500 W ([Wikipedia: Granite Rapids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite_Rapids)). Its AMX units give an architectural peak of 2,048 INT8 operations per cycle per core ([Intel Architecture Day 2021 claims](https://www.intel.com/ArchDay21claims)), which at the 2.0–3.2 GHz base-to-all-core-turbo range works out to **524–839 INT8 TOPS per socket (peak, my arithmetic; Intel does not publish the sustained AMX clock)**, five times AMD's 192-core EPYC 9965 at 111–182 TOPS peak and $14,813 list ([Wikipedia: Epyc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epyc)). AMD's socket matches Intel on bandwidth, and Chips and Cheese measured a Turin 9575F reading at **99 % of its 576 GB/s theoretical** ([Chips and Cheese](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/amds-turin-5th-gen-epyc-launched)), but it lacks AMX, and every fast CPU LLM runtime found in this research (SGLang CPU, ktransformers, IPEX) requires AMX.

What those sockets deliver end to end is far below the theoretical figures. The best documented CPU-only result is Intel's and LMSYS's SGLang CPU backend on a dual 6980P with 1.5 TB of MRDIMM-8800: **DeepSeek-R1-671B INT8 at 14.7 tok/s decode and about 543 tok/s prefill, Llama-70B INT8 at 13.1 tok/s decode** (measured July 2025, batch 1, vendor-authored), with llama.cpp on the same box 2.5–4x slower at decode and 13x slower at prefill ([LMSYS/Intel blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2025-07-14-intel-xeon-optimization)). Those rates imply an effective decode bandwidth of about 915 GB/s on the dense model and 544 GB/s on the MoE model out of 1.69 TB/s theoretical, **54 % and 32 % utilisation**, which is the range the simulator uses for every Xeon scenario. Community llama.cpp runs confirm the pattern on AMD: a single EPYC 9175F streams Llama-3.1-70B F16 at 2.35 tok/s and a second socket adds only 1.0–1.8x depending on NUMA tricks ([llama.cpp discussion #11733](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/11733)). Lenovo's July 2026 vLLM study shows the CPU's real niche: a 96-core Xeon 6972P serves Llama-3.1-8B BF16 at about 30 tok/s per user for up to eight users and reaches **8 million output tokens per kWh at 128 users** ([Lenovo LP2463](https://lenovopress.lenovo.com/lp2463.pdf)), good efficiency for small models and many users, not for dense 70B at interactive speed.

The cost side moved against CPUs during 2026. A 64 GB DDR5 RDIMM went from about $255 in Q3 2025 to over $900 contract in Q1 2026 ([Network World citing Counterpoint](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4093752/server-memory-prices-could-double-by-2026-as-ai-demand-strains-supply.html)), and retail server DDR5 sat at a **median $35.70/GB in August 2026** ([Capital & Compute](https://capitalandcompute.net/memory-prices/server/)) and **$44.33/GB in the October 2026 index** ([DatacenterDisk](https://datacenterdisk.com/server-ram/price-index)). Filling a 12-channel socket with 64 GB modules therefore costs $11,000–25,000, more than the CPU, and the whole socket lands at **$40–60 per GB/s of theoretical bandwidth**. GPUs were also hit by the memory shortage, which doubled the RTX PRO 6000 from $8,565 at launch to $16,000 on NVIDIA's own store ([TechPowerUp](https://www.techpowerup.com/351549/nvidia-rtx-pro-6000-blackwell-96-gb-gpu-now-costs-usd-16-000)), pushed the RTX 5090 to $7,444 new against a $1,999 MSRP ([VideoCardPrices](https://videocardprices.com/card/nvidia-rtx-5090/)) and raised H100 rental medians 24 % year on year to $3.70/hr ([GetDeploying](https://getdeploying.com/gpu-price-trends)). Even so, the cheapest reliable H100 rental is about **$1.99/hr for 3.35 TB/s** and the B200 in-stock floor about $3.75/hr for 8 TB/s ([dreaming.press](https://dreaming.press/posts/gpu-rental-price-october-2026.html)), and a used RTX 3090 still sells for $699–1,189 ([ShopBack](https://www.shopback.com/blog/electronics/nvidia-rtx-5090-supply-shortage-2026-us-what-to-buy-instead)).

FPGAs sit in the worst corner of this map. The AI-engine parts that have real matrix throughput ship without high-bandwidth memory, and the HBM parts ship without AI engines: the Alveo V80 offers **32 GB HBM2e at 819–820 GB/s, PCIe Gen5, four 200G ports, 190 W, $9,495 list with a 10-week lead time**, but its compute is DSP fabric only and its shipped flow is Vivado RTL ([AMD Alveo V80](https://www.amd.com/en/products/accelerators/alveo/v80/a-v80-p64g-pq-g.html)); Altera's Agilex 7 M-series dev kit pairs 820 GB/s of HBM2e with a vendor-theoretical 88.5 INT8 TOPS at €9,889 and a **33-week factory lead time** ([Mouser](https://www.mouser.com.tr/en/c/semiconductors/engineering-development-tools/programmable-logic-ic-development-tools/?pg=14), [Altera FPGA AI Suite](https://www.mouser.com.tr/altera-fpga-ai-suite)); AMD's Versal AI Edge Gen 2 projects up to 184 dense INT8 TOPS but kits were backordered in 2026 ([AMD Gen 2 page](https://www.amd.com/en/products/adaptive-socs-and-fpgas/versal/gen2/ai-edge-series.html)); and the one card that combined 404 INT8 TOPS with a data-centre form factor, the Alveo V70, is discontinued ([AMD Alveo V70](https://www.amd.com/en/products/accelerators/alveo/v70/a-v70-p16g-pq-g.html)). Independent measurements land at 60–70 % of peak for tuned GEMM kernels (MaxEVA: 77 INT8 TOPS on a 133-TOPS VC1902) and 1–16 % end to end ([MaxEVA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04980v2), [CHARM](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10544221-charm-composing-heterogeneous-accelerators-deep-learning-versal-acap-architecture)), so the planning number for an AI-engine FPGA is 30–80 sustained INT8 TOPS at **$39–99 per peak TOPS** versus $12–18 for an H100.

## The fabric fixes a 2 ms term inside a 32 ms token

The physics is the roofline. A decode step at batch 1 is a matrix-vector product with an arithmetic intensity of 1–4 FLOP per byte depending on weight precision, far below the ridge point of any chip (about 295 FLOP/byte for an H100 in BF16, 80–100 for a DDR5 Xeon), so step time is bytes read divided by memory bandwidth ([Williams et al., Roofline](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1498765.1498785), [kipply](https://kipp.ly/transformer-inference-arithmetic/)). Pope et al. state it plainly: at small batch "the time to load weights dominates" ([Pope et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102)), and Databricks measures well-tuned GPU serving at 50–60 % of peak bandwidth at batch 1 ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/llm-inference-performance-engineering-best-practices)). For Llama-3.3-70B the per-token read is **40 GB at 4-bit, 70.6 GB at INT8 and 141 GB at BF16**, plus 320 KiB of KV cache per token of context; for DeepSeek-V3/R1 it is about 21 GB at 4-bit of a 377 GB resident model ([Llama 3 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783), [DeepSeek-V3 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437)). Ten tokens per second on 70B 4-bit therefore needs 400 GB/s of delivered bandwidth and 100 tok/s needs 4 TB/s, which is one H100's HBM or seven to nine DDR5 sockets at the measured 450–600 GB/s each, all reading their shard of the weights at the same instant.

That last condition is the only thing a sync fabric can affect. Splitting a model across sockets by layers (pipeline parallelism, what llama.cpp RPC does) adds capacity but no batch-1 speed, because each token still walks the whole model one stage at a time; the measured results are uniformly negative, a 72B model slowing from 11.1 to 5.9 tok/s when split over 10 GbE ([kjaiswal benchmarks](https://github.com/kjaiswal/llama-cpp-distributed-benchmarks)) and Qwen3-235B slowing from 20.4 to 15.2 tok/s across four Mac Studios ([Jeff Geerling](https://www.jeffgeerling.com/blog/2025/15-tb-vram-on-mac-studio-rdma-over-thunderbolt-5)). Only Megatron-style tensor parallelism makes N sockets read concurrently, and it costs **two all-reduces per layer, 160 per token for an 80-layer 70B model**, each on a vector of only 16 KiB at batch 1 ([Megatron-LM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053), [prima.cpp](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08791v2)). The bytes are trivial, 2.5–4.4 MiB per node per token; the latency is not, because a ring all-reduce takes 2(N−1) sequential link traversals and a token therefore waits on 320 (N=2) to 2,240 (N=8) of them. On on-package links such as Intel UPI 2.0 at 48 GB/s per direction and roughly 200 ns remote-memory latency ([Wikipedia: UPI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_Ultra_Path_Interconnect), [Lenovo xGMI measurements](https://lenovopress.lenovo.com/lp1852-configuring-amd-xgmi-links-on-thinksystem-sr665-v3)) the floor is 0.16–1.1 ms per token, which matches SGLang's measured **3 % all-reduce overhead across two 6980P sockets** ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2025-07-14-intel-xeon-optimization)); on RDMA at 1–3 µs per hop it is 1–7 ms; on kernel TCP over 10 GbE at about 50 µs it is 16–112 ms; on Wi-Fi it is seconds, which is why prima.cpp reports about 30 s per token for a naive 70B split over four home devices ([prima.cpp](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08791v2)).

So the FPGA's one legitimate job is to collapse the O(N) ring into an O(1) tree: every host DMAs its 16 KiB vector into the card, an adder tree reduces the four streams as they arrive, and the result DMAs back, **two fixed hops per collective and 320 per token regardless of N**, about 0.6 ms per token at 2 µs per hop and 1.6 ms at 5 µs. The floor on that hop is the host interface, not the FPGA logic: a PCIe DMA round trip is about 1 µs, and a CXL Type-2 FPGA sees **590–620 ns per uncached 64-byte host read** against 140–410 ns for ASIC CXL devices and about 100 ns for local DRAM ([Hitchhiker's Guide to CXL/NVLink-C2C](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.02814), [CXL over Ethernet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.08055)). FPGA-implemented links are consistently 1.3–2x slower than the ASIC NICs they would replace. The best academic precedent, ACCL+ at OSDI 2024 on ten Alveo U55C cards, found exactly this: lower latency when the data already lives in the FPGA, but for CPU-generated data "comparable performance to software RDMA MPI while freeing up CPU cycles", and for reductions on host data "only marginal benefits and, in some cases, falls short of software MPI" ([ACCL+](https://usenix.org/system/files/osdi24-he.pdf)). The strongest evidence that link latency can matter is the exo cluster result, where cutting Thunderbolt latency from about 300 µs to under 50 µs raised a 1T-parameter model from 5 to 25 tok/s on four M3 Ultras ([Creative Strategies](https://creativestrategies.com/research/running-a-1t-parameter-model-on-a-40k-mac-studio-cluster/)), but that is the regime of a slow link being replaced by RDMA, which a ConnectX-7-class NIC at about $2,500 per host (the prototype's bill-of-materials estimate) already provides.

Put the two terms side by side for the proposed cluster and the verdict writes itself. Four 6980P hosts each read 10 GB per token of a 70B 4-bit model; at the measured 32–54 % of 614 GB/s that is **about 32 ms at the median**, and the simulator's layer-by-layer walk puts the all-reduces at 2.0 ms over 400G RDMA and 1.0 ms through the FPGA tree ([docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md](../docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md)). The fabric removes 0.9 ms from a 34 ms step, a 3 % gain, and even a hypothetical 0.3 µs host hop, which neither PCIe nor measured CXL can deliver, would only cut the term to about 0.4 ms. Intel's own eight-socket tensor-parallel run shows the same shape at larger scale: Llama-2-70B went from 249.7 ms per token on two Xeon 8563C sockets to 87.7 ms on eight across four machines, 2.85x on 4x the hardware, and a bandwidth model predicts 58 ms, so roughly a third of the eight-socket step is communication and software overhead that a perfect fabric could at best halve ([He et al., arXiv 2407.07304](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07304)).

What remains after the fabric is fixed is everything that actually matters. First, **DRAM bandwidth per socket** (0.6–0.85 TB/s theoretical against 3.35–8 TB/s per GPU) and its 32–54 % realised utilisation under current CPU runtimes; the FPGA adds no bytes per second, and an FPGA reading host DRAM over PCIe Gen5 x16 gets at most about 60 GB/s per direction, a tenth of one socket ([Atomic Rules Gen5 DMA, vendor](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20241118580904/en/Atomic-Rules-Ships-Arkville%C2%AE-24.11-Adding-PCIe-Gen5-DMA-to-AMD-Versal%E2%84%A2-Gen-1-Devices)). Second, **DRAM cost**, which dominates the bill of materials and scales linearly with sockets. Third, **prefill compute**: the measured 388–543 tok/s prefill on two 6980P sockets implies about 27 TFLOPS per socket, roughly 5 % of the AMX peak, so a 4,096-token prompt that an H100 prefills in about a second takes 7.5–10.5 s on the pair, and no interconnect changes that ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2025-07-14-intel-xeon-optimization), [docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md](../docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md)). Fourth, the **CPU ridge point**: at 80–100 FLOP/byte a DDR5 socket leaves the bandwidth-bound regime at batch 25–50 and then caps at a few hundred aggregate tok/s on 70B, so batching, the lever that makes GPUs cheap per token, runs out early on CPUs. Fifth, **multi-host software**, where measured scaling is 71 % efficient at best (Intel's 8 sockets) and negative in llama.cpp RPC. And sixth, the FPGA supply chain itself: 8–33 week lead times, Vitis AI having dropped support for the U50, U280 and VCK5000 within three years ([AMD UG1354 v3.5](https://docs.amd.com/r/en-US/ug1354-xilinx-ai-sdk/Vitis-AI-Library-v3.5-Release-Notes)), the V70 discontinued, and Microsoft's retrospective on the largest AI-FPGA deployment ever built, Brainwave, concluding it was "competitive (but generally inferior) on performance metrics compared to GPUs", that software optimisation shrank one FPGA speedup from 250x to 30x in months, and that "FPGAs didn't win the HW lottery" ([Putnam, NANDA 2024 keynote](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~phjk/NANDA24/Slides/Catapult_Putnam_NANDA_2024_Keynote_Public_pdf.pdf)). Intel bought Altera for about $16.7 B in 2015 and sold control in 2025 at an $8.75 B valuation after a $615 M operating loss on $1.54 B of revenue, during the largest AI capital boom in history ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/14/intel-agrees-to-sell-controlling-stake-in-altera-chip-business)).

## The Intel prototype: four revisions, and the simulator's verdict

The design document [docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md](../docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md) builds the idea with real parts in four revisions and scores each with [tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py](../tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py), a dependency-free Monte-Carlo simulator that walks a transformer layer by layer (QKV, attention over KV, output projection, all-reduce, MLP up and down, all-reduce), models the fabric path as host DMA, serialisation, reduction hops and completion, samples every uncertain parameter from a range, and reports P10–P90 bands. Costs assume three-year amortisation at 50 % utilisation and $0.15/kWh with no hosting fees. The lumped roofline companion [tools/inference_cost_model.py](../tools/inference_cost_model.py) accepts a `--custom` device and link on the command line for paper checks of other configurations. What follows summarises the document; the full tables for four models are in [docs/sim_results.md](../docs/sim_results.md), and this report does not re-derive them.

**Rev 0** is the observation that Intel already sells the sync fabric for two CPUs. A dual-socket Xeon 6900P board joins the sockets with UPI 2.0 at 48 GB/s per direction per link and about 200 ns latency, and SGLang measured the resulting all-reduce overhead at 3 %, which no card on a PCIe slot can beat. The build is two 6980P at about $14,000 implied street each, 24 DDR5-6400 RDIMMs to fill all channels (24 × 16 GB at $560 is the cheapest way to get the whole 1.23 TB/s; 32 GB MRDIMM-8800 modules are estimated at $45/GB because no public price exists), and an estimated $5,000 chassis, for **about $47,000 with DDR5 or $68,000 with MRDIMM**. The measured anchors on exactly this hardware are the LMSYS numbers above, 13.1 tok/s on 70B INT8 and 14.7 tok/s on R1 INT8; the simulator's band for the MRDIMM box is 8–17 and 16–32 tok/s respectively.

**Rev 1** builds the thing the FPGA would replace: four single-socket 6980P hosts with 192 GB each, a 400G RoCE NIC per host (ConnectX-7 class, about $2,500, 0.5–1.5 µs adapter latency), six direct-attach cables in a full mesh so no switch adds hops, and a rack, **about $116,000 and 2.9 kW**, running oneCCL tensor parallelism under xFasterTransformer or SGLang. Intel's precedent is Qwen-72B at 140 ms per token on four Xeon 8575C sockets ([He et al., arXiv 2407.00029](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00029v1)). The simulator gives **20–39 tok/s (P50 30) on 70B 4-bit at batch 1, with all-reduces 6 % of the step**.

**Rev 2** is the proposal: replace each NIC with an FPGA card that performs the reduction in hardware over direct optical links. The part is the Alveo V80 (PCIe Gen5, four QSFP56 200G ports allowing a switchless four-node mesh, 32 GB HBM2e, 190 W, $9,495 list) or, for an Intel-native build, an Altera Agilex 7 I-series card such as the BittWare IA-840f at an estimated $10,000 since no public price exists; total hardware is **about $136,000 and 3.2 kW**, plus non-recurring engineering. The RTL that must be written is a host DMA engine with doorbell and completion path, a BF16/FP32 adder tree over four streaming 16 KiB vectors, a lightweight link layer over the QSFP ports (Microsoft's Catapult LTL measured 2.88 µs FPGA-to-FPGA through a top-of-rack switch, so direct links should be about 1 µs one way ([Catapult v2](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Cloud-Scale-Acceleration-Architecture.pdf))), and a oneCCL plug-in on the CPU side. The document's estimate is two FPGA engineers and one systems engineer for 9–15 months, **$0.5–1.5 M (estimate; the notes found no month-level effort data, and ACCL+ was a multi-year research project)**. The simulator gives **21–41 tok/s (P50 30), all-reduces 3 %**, with weight streaming at 31.8 ms and all-reduces at 1.0 ms per token at the median, against 2.0 ms in Rev 1. Three reworks are scored: 2b attaches the FPGA as a CXL Type-2 device so the CPU writes vectors with plain stores (21–42 tok/s, under 1 % better, because measured FPGA CXL access is 0.4–0.7 µs); 2c overlaps the all-reduce of one micro-batch with the compute of the next (+3 % at batch 1, more at larger batch, and a pure software change that works just as well with NICs); 2d doubles to eight hosts (39–74 tok/s at $273,000, with no improvement in cost per token because the DRAM scales with the sockets).

**Rev 3** is the only FPGA architecture with a physics case: an HBM FPGA holds a shard of the weights and streams them itself at 820 GB/s, 1.3x a Xeon socket's theoretical bandwidth at a third of the memory cost. Four Agilex 7 M-series HBM2e kits (or four Alveo V80s, which lack AI engines and are estimated at about 20 DSP INT8 TOPS), four host PCs and cables come to **about $88,000 and 1.8 kW**, with an estimated **$2–5 M of engineering** to write a full transformer engine, the class of work FlightLLM, DFX and EdgeLLM each represent as multi-year academic efforts. The simulator gives **31–59 tok/s on 70B 4-bit**; the band is wide because published FPGA LLM engines reach about 35 % of HBM bandwidth (FlightLLM: 55 tok/s on a U280 and 92.5 tok/s on a VHK158 for 7B models at about 3.5 bits, against 197 tok/s for gpt-fast INT4 on an A100 ([FlightLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.03868v2))) while Positron claims 93 % on the same Agilex-M silicon with no independent measurement ([EE Times Asia](https://www.eetasia.com/startup-takes-on-nvidia-with-fpga-based-ai/)). Even at the top of the band this is a used-GPU-pair result at twelve times the price, and Positron, having raised $875 M at a $5 B valuation in September 2026, is taping out an ASIC to replace the FPGA ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/positron-ai-raises-875m-at-a-5bn-valuation-to-support-develop-of-next-generation-hardware/)).

### How far to trust the simulator

Running `python3 tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py --validate` compares the model with seven measured batch-1 points. The dual-6980P MRDIMM pair on 70B INT8 measured 13.1 tok/s against a simulated band of about 8–17 (P50 about 12.5); the same box on R1 INT8 measured 14.7 against about 16–32, just below the band, which is the MoE routing and attention overhead the simulator does not charge for; the RTX 5090 and 3090 llama.cpp results, the M3 Ultra on R1 4-bit, Strix Halo and DGX Spark on 70B Q4 all fall inside or at the edge of their bands ([docs/sim_results.md](../docs/sim_results.md)). The model is deliberately generous to the CPU side: perfect load balance, no NUMA mistakes, and a fixed 8–25 µs of software overhead per layer, where real multi-host CPU runs scale worse (Intel's own 8-socket run reached 71 % efficiency, and llama.cpp RPC gets slower with more nodes). The one place the FPGA could surprise the model is the host hop, and even a 0.3 µs hop, below anything measured over PCIe or CXL, would take the all-reduce term from 1.0 to about 0.4 ms per token, still 1–2 % of the step. Numbers the notes could not source and that should be verified before any purchase are listed in section 7 of the design document: MRDIMM-8800 module and dual-socket 6900P board prices, the V80 port and lane configuration, Agilex 7 I-series card prices, the sustained all-core AMX clock, any independent Positron benchmark, and 2026 Gaudi 3 pricing.

## A $1,500 rental decides the question before any FPGA is bought

The value of the prototype document is that the proposal can be falsified for almost nothing, because its premise, that inter-socket communication is a large share of the token time, is measurable on rented hardware with existing software. The plan is a sequence of gates in which Rev 2 is funded only if gate 2 shows communication is a large share of the token time and gate 3 shows the FPGA can cut it; the simulator predicts that gate 2 fails by a factor of three and gate 3 is marginal ([docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md](../docs/prototype_intel_fpga_cluster.md)).

| Gate | Cost | What to run | Pass criterion | Simulator prediction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Paper | $0 | `python3 tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py` with your own prices; a `--custom` scenario in `tools/inference_cost_model.py` | any CPU configuration under $10 per million tokens at batch 16 | none exists at 2026 DRAM prices |
| 1. One box, rented | ~$500–1,500 for a week | a dual-socket Xeon 6900P bare-metal server, SGLang CPU backend, Llama-70B INT8 and 8B INT8 at batch 1 and 16; record decode and prefill tok/s and `perf` memory-bandwidth counters | end-to-end memory-bandwidth utilisation above 60 % | 32–54 % (measured 2025) |
| 2. Two hosts, RDMA | ~$5k–50k (rent or buy) | two single-socket hosts with ConnectX-7 direct-attached, xFasterTransformer TP=2; measure 16 KiB all-reduce latency with oneCCL benchmarks and the end-to-end token time | communication above 20 % of the token time (otherwise there is nothing for an FPGA to fix) | 6 % at four hosts |
| 3. FPGA latency only | ~$20k | two Alveo V80 or Agilex 7 I-series cards, a minimal DMA-reduce-DMA loop, no model | host-to-host all-reduce of 16 KiB under 2 µs | 2–4 µs (PCIe DMA floor about 1 µs each way) |
| 4. Full Rev 2 | ~$136k plus NRE | only if gates 2 and 3 both pass | beats Rev 1 by more than 25 % tok/s at batch 1 | +3 % |

Gate 1 is the one most worth running regardless of the FPGA decision, because it measures the lever that would actually move CPU inference: utilisation. Xeon decode currently realises 32–54 % of theoretical bandwidth while GPU serving stacks reach 70–85 %, and closing that gap is worth up to 1.5–2x with no new hardware; it is Intel's and the SGLang team's problem, and progress on it should be tracked before any capital is committed. Gate 1 also exposes the prefill problem directly, since the measured AMX efficiency of about 5 % of peak means that better kernels could give 3–6x on prompt processing, again in software. Gate 2 has a measured proxy already: SGLang's 3 % overhead across two sockets on UPI and the 6 % the simulator predicts over RDMA at four hosts. If a real measurement on two RDMA-connected hosts shows communication under 10 % of the token time, the FPGA fabric has a ceiling of a 10 % gain and the project should stop there. The same gates apply to an AMD-based build, with the caveat that without AMX the only tensor-parallel CPU runtime in the notes is llama.cpp, whose dual-socket scaling is 1.0–1.8x.

## Nearby designs that do deliver cheaper inference

The pattern behind every design that works is the same: put the weights behind the cheapest memory that has the bandwidth, let the CPU contribute capacity rather than bandwidth, and buy compute only where prefill or batching needs it. The proven version is the **ktransformers split**, in which a dual-socket Xeon holds DeepSeek-R1's 382 GB of 4-bit experts in DRAM and one 24 GB GPU runs attention and the dense layers: 12–14 tok/s decode and up to 287 tok/s prefill on two Xeon Gold 6454S plus an RTX 4090D, with the AMX MoE kernel lifting prefill 3.45x over the non-AMX version ([ktransformers tutorial](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/main/doc/en/DeepseekR1_V3_tutorial.md)). This is the same CPU-plus-accelerator layout that Nvidia itself now sells as GPU-prefill plus Groq-LPU-decode and that Alibaba's XuanTie C950 server CPU is built around, and in 2025 DRAM prices it cost $6,000–12,000 of CPU and memory; the FPGA fabric would have to beat that box on price or tokens per second to be interesting, and it does neither.

For dense models up to 70B at interactive speed the answer is **used consumer GPUs**. Two used RTX 3090s at $1,400–2,400 of cards decode 70B 4-bit at a simulated 25–33 tok/s, two used 4090s at 29–36 tok/s, and a measured three-3090 build runs gpt-oss-120B at 124 tok/s, faster than a $4,699 DGX Spark at 38.6 ([Hardware Corner](https://www.hardware-corner.net/first-dgx-spark-llm-benchmarks/)). On rental the same cards are 2–4x cheaper per unit of bandwidth than data-centre GPUs: an RTX 4090 at $0.34/hr community rate on RunPod against an H100 at $2.69 ([RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)). For a model that must sit in one box with more than 32 GB, the **Mac Studio M5 Ultra** is the strongest single-machine option: 1.2 TB/s, $5,499 for 96 GB, measured 179 tok/s on 7B Q4_0 and 119 on 7B Q8 ([llama.cpp Apple scoreboard](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/4167)), a simulated 14–20 tok/s on 70B 4-bit and **$0.61–0.98 per million tokens at batch 16**, within a factor of two of an H100 at a fifth of the price, with 256 GB at $9,499–10,799 ([Invide Labs](https://blog.invidelabs.com/m5-ultra-mac-studio-local-ai/)). The 128 GB Strix Halo mini PCs at $1,999–2,851 are the cheapest capacity on the market and decode MoE models well (gpt-oss-120B MXFP4 at 47.5 tok/s measured in llama.cpp ([llama.cpp discussion #16578](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/16578))) but dense 70B at only about 5 tok/s.

Inside Intel's own catalogue the answer to the question is **Gaudi 3**, not a Xeon cluster: 128 GB of HBM2e at 3.7 TB/s and $15,625 per chip at the 2024 kit price (no 2026 price was found), simulated at 56–70 tok/s on 70B 4-bit and **$0.58–0.73 per million tokens at batch 16**, the best cost per token of any purchasable part in the table bar the B200 ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/intel-xeon-6-e-cores-now-available-company-reveals-pricing-for-gaudi-ai-accelerator), [docs/sim_results.md](../docs/sim_results.md)). For 8B-class models an **Intel Arc B580** at $579–655 street gives 31–40 tok/s on 8B INT8 for $1.4–1.7 per million tokens ([GPUPriceHistory](https://gpupricehistory.com/us/arc-b580/vs/rx-9070-xt)). And the dual-socket Xeon 6 box of Rev 0 remains the right tool for one job: a model that fits no affordable accelerator when privacy or data residency rules out an API, where DeepSeek-R1 INT8 at 14.7 tok/s with 768 GB of memory is a real capability, just not a cheap one.

If the goal is cost per token rather than ownership, nothing built in 2026 beats renting batched GPU capacity or buying tokens. Below roughly 40–50 % utilisation a per-token API is cheaper than renting a GPU at all ([dreaming.press](https://dreaming.press/posts/gpu-rental-price-october-2026.html)), open-model APIs are priced within about 2x of batched GPU cost, and the price of a fixed level of capability has been falling 5–10x per year at the frontier and up to 40x per year for GPT-4-level performance ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)). A hardware project with an 18–24 month build cycle therefore launches against token prices 30–100x lower than today's. The software levers are also larger than anything a fabric offers: 4-bit weights cut bytes per token 3.6x against BF16, speculative decoding gives 2–3x per-user speed with identical outputs and is the only lever that lifts batch-1 tokens per second above bandwidth divided by bytes ([Leviathan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)), mixture-of-experts cuts active bytes 18x for DeepSeek, and a 1.58-bit 70B model would be about 14 GB and run at about 30 tok/s on one DDR5 socket if a trained one existed, which as of the bitnet.cpp paper it does not beyond 2–8B ([BitNet b1.58, arXiv 2410.16144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16144)).

Two FPGA-adjacent directions deserve a mention because they are where the fabric idea has real commercial value. The first is **Rev 3 as a business**: Positron's Atlas appliance on Agilex 7 M-series HBM FPGAs claims that two Atlas cards deliver almost double the tokens/s of two H100s on Llama-3.1-8B at about a third of the power ([EE Times Asia, vendor claims](https://www.eetasia.com/startup-takes-on-nvidia-with-fpga-based-ai/), [BittWare-Molex Positron Atlas](https://www.bittware-molex.com/products/positron-atlas/)), has a 50-rack deployment at Oracle Cloud, and has attracted $875 M to move to a 2 TB-per-accelerator ASIC ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/positron-ai-raises-875m-at-a-5bn-valuation-to-support-develop-of-next-generation-hardware/)); the lesson is that the FPGA was a prototype vehicle for a memory-centric decode chip, not the product. The second is the **CXL memory-fabric direction**, where a fabric chip adds bandwidth and capacity rather than lower latency: Enfabrica's ACF-S targets 8 Tbps of chip bandwidth and pairs with CXL DDR5 pools, and NVIDIA paid more than $900 M for its CEO and a technology licence ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2024/11/19/enfabrica-raises-115m-debuts-worlds-fastest-gpu-networking-chip/)). Its customers attach GPUs to the pool; nobody attaches CPUs to each other, and the history of machines that did, from 32-socket NUMAlink Superdomes (an 8-socket system at $2.5 M in a 2022 TPC-H disclosure ([TPC-H report](https://www.tpc.org/results/individual_results/HPE/hpe~tpch~100000~hpe_superdome_flex_280_server~es~2022-03-21~v02.pdf))) to Xeon Phi's 2018 end of life for lack of demand against GPUs ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/966180/intel-ends-the-xeon-phi-product-line.html)) to Esperanto's thousand-RISC-V-core inference chip winding down in 2025 ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/ai-startup-esperanto-winds-down-silicon-business/)), says that glue which makes many general-purpose parts look like one bigger part sells at a premium into niches and does not displace a purpose-built accelerator whose advantage is on-package bandwidth.

## Conclusion

The proposal reads as a compute problem, "CPUs are slow at matrix maths, so synchronise enough of them", but inference in 2026 is a memory-streaming problem, and the analysis reframes the question as one about who sells bytes per second most cheaply. On that scale the ranking is fixed by the memory technology, not the processor: GDDR on consumer cards at about $1–4 per GB/s, LPDDR5X on Apple's package at about $4.5, HBM on data-centre parts at $4–12, and DDR5 RDIMMs on a 12-channel socket at $40–60, with 2026's DRAM shortage widening rather than closing the gap. A sync fabric sits on top of that ranking and cannot change it; the FPGA's measurable contribution, about one millisecond per token, is smaller than the run-to-run noise of the CPU runtimes it would serve. The design document and simulator in this repository are therefore most useful as a reusable instrument: change the DRAM price, the utilisation or the socket count in `tools/sim_xeon_fpga_cluster.py` and the model shows exactly how far the market would have to move before a CPU cluster beats a used GPU, and it has not moved there in any scenario the notes can source.

The productive reading of the idea is the one Intel, Tsinghua's ktransformers team and Positron have already converged on: the CPU is the cheap place to keep hundreds of gigabytes of sparsely activated weights, an accelerator with real bandwidth does the streaming and the prompt arithmetic, and the link between them carries routed activations rather than synchronisation traffic. If the goal is to build something, build gate 1 and gate 2 of the test plan for under $2,000 of rental, measure utilisation and communication share on real Xeon 6 hardware, and let those two numbers, not a fabric, decide what to buy next.
